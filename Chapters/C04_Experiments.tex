\chapter{Experiments} \label{ch:exp}
%************************************************

\Cref{ch:methods} derived the \ac{VAEE} as a probabilistic
generative model but left its empirical behaviour open. This chapter puts the
architecture's central claim to the test --- that representing a concept by a
vector rather than a scalar relaxes the sparsity--reconstruction trade-off that
bounds \acp{SAE} (\cref{sec:methods:aim}) --- on
one application of it: the decomposition of \ac{LLM} activations.

\Cref{sec:exp:setup} describes the activation
dataset, drawn from the residual stream of a pretrained \ac{LLM}.
\Cref{sec:exp:baselines} introduces the
\ac{SAE} baseline against which the \ac{VAEE} is compared at matched parameter
count. \Cref{sec:exp:metrics} sets
out the evaluation metrics --- a measure of reconstruction fidelity and two of
sparsity. \Cref{sec:exp:training} reports
the training setup, and \cref{sec:exp:results}
presents the quantitative, qualitative, and ablation
results.\graffito{Review this paragraph after whole chapter is written.}

\section{Experiment setup} \label{sec:exp:setup}

The \ac{VAEE} operates on a single activation vector $\vb{x} \in \R^{n}$
(\cref{sec:methods:notation}), so evaluating it on a
language model requires a corpus of such vectors. These were extracted from the
residual stream of \texttt{Mistral 7B v0.1} \citep{jiangMistral7B2023}, a
pretrained decoder-only \ac{LLM}, run over SetFit's version\footnote{Available
for download at \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/SetFit/sst2}.} of the
\ac{SST2} sentiment classification dataset
\citep{socherRecursiveDeepModels2013}.

\ac{SST2} is a dataset of movie reviews, each with a label (positive/negative)
attached. For the purposes of the experiments we were only interested in the
reviews as a corpus of natural language, so the labels were discarded. \ac{SST2}
is a small corpus: large enough to exercise reconstruction, but, in hindsight,
probably too narrow for a large and varied concept dictionary to emerge, a
limitation returned to when the results are interpreted
(\cref{sec:exp:results}). This problem was
exacerbated by the choice of SetFit's version instead of the original version of
the dataset, since the former contains only $6\,920$ reviews in the training
set, while the latter has $67\,300$ reviews in the training set. The total
number of tokens in each set is shown in \cref{tab:exp:dataset}.

Each review was passed through the model and its residual-stream state was read
off after the final transformer block, just before the classifier head. This
yielded the residual-stream representation of every token in the sentence. The
resulting activations have dimension $n = 4\,096$, \ie Mistral's residual-stream
dimension.

\section{Baselines} \label{sec:exp:baselines}

The \ac{VAEE} is compared against a TopK \ac{SAE}
(\cref{sec:background:ae:sae})
\citep{gaoScalingEvaluatingSparse2024}, the state-of-the-art model for \ac{MI}.
Its top-$k$ activation fixes the number of active features per sample directly
through $k$, so the reconstruction error can be read against a sparsity level
that is set rather than induced by a penalty weight.
Sweeping $k$ traces the baseline's sparsity--reconstruction frontier, against
which the \ac{VAEE}'s operating point is placed.

The two models are matched on the size of their encoder and decoder rather than
on dictionary size, so that any difference reflects how each represents a
concept and not how much capacity it is given. Both map the activation through
an encoder into a latent code and back through a decoder; that code has $m$
entries for \iac{SAE} and $K \cdot d$ for \iac{VAEE} --- its $K$ concepts of
embedding dimension $d$, written $K \times d$ --- and the encoder and decoder
weight matrices are sized to match. Matching therefore sets $K \cdot d = m$: the
$128 \times 64$ \ac{VAEE} is paired with the $m = 8\,192$ \ac{SAE}, $64
\times 64$ with $m = 4\,096$, and $32 \times 64$ with $m =
2\,048$.\footnote{This equalises the encoder and decoder weights, which dominate
    the parameter count ($\approx 2 n \cdot K d$ for both models). Beyond them
    the \ac{VAEE} carries a handful of parameters with no \ac{SAE} counterpart
    --- its $K$ learnable prototypes, of dimension $d$, and the gate temperature
    --- so exact matching of the total would pair the three \ac{VAEE} sizes with
    \acp{SAE} of $m = 8\,194$, $4\,097$, and $2\,049$ rather than the powers of
    two used here. The surplus is one or two dictionary atoms, under $0.05\%$ of
the count, so the totals agree to well within a percent.} The \ac{SAE}'s
dictionary is correspondingly far larger than the \ac{VAEE}'s concept count ---
each \ac{VAEE} concept spends $d$ parameters where \iac{SAE} atom spends one.

\section{Evaluation Metrics} \label{sec:exp:metrics}

Two properties are measured: how faithfully a model reconstructs an activation,
and how sparse the decomposition it uses to do so is. Reconstruction is reported
as a single error metric; sparsity is reported in two ways, because the
\ac{VAEE} and the \ac{SAE} do not count activity in the same unit.

\subsection{Reconstruction} Reconstruction fidelity is measured by the \ac{MSE}
between an activation and its reconstruction, averaged across the whole dataset.
This is the same quantity defined in
\cref{eq:background:ae:recon-loss}. It is the natural choice
here: under the Gaussian decoder of \cref{eq:methods:proba:likelihood}
the \ac{VAEE}'s reconstruction term reduces to exactly this
error (\cref{sec:methods:model:loss}),
and it is the standard reconstruction measure for \acp{SAE}, so the two models
are scored on the objective each is trained to optimise.\footnote{The two
reconstruction checks that are standard for \acp{SAE} spliced back into a
language model --- the \ac{CE} loss and perplexity of the patched model --- are
not reported for this comparison, as they were not run for the \ac{VAEE};
\ac{MSE} is the only reconstruction metric on which both models are evaluated
here.}

Evaluating reconstruction is important, as a model that has low reconstruction
error is a model that has learned \textit{representative} features of the input
data, and concepts are representative features by definition.

\subsection{Sparsity}

    A low active-concept count is not the same as a sparse code. The \ac{VAEE}
    targets the number of concepts active per sample, not the fraction of its
    dictionary left idle, so at higher $\kappa$ it may fire a sizable share of
    its concepts without contradicting its aim. Dictionary-fraction sparsity is
    the \ac{SAE}'s design principle, not the \ac{VAEE}'s; the quantity that
    bears on the comparison is the absolute number of active units, reported
    below in two ways.

    \paragraph{Number of active concepts $\kappa$} From an interpretability
    standpoint, the central sparsity metric is the number of active concepts:
    this is the number of units a human must understand in order to explain the
    input instance. For a TopK \ac{SAE} this corresponds to its $k$
    hyperparameter by
    construction;\footnote{\label{fn:exp:metrics:topk}Technically, TopK selects
        the $k$ largest activations, but it is possible that after the ReLU only
        $k' < k$ activations are non-zero, so $\ell_{0} = \kappa = k' < k$. In
        practice, this almost never happens, but the $\ell_{0}$ is recomputed
    every time for maximum accuracy nonetheless.} for \iac{VAEE}, $\kappa =
    \#\{k : \alpha_k(\vb{x}) \ge \flatfrac{1}{2}\}$, the count of concepts whose
    posterior gate exceeds a threshold. Although the inference gate is
    continuous
    (\cref{sec:methods:model:proba:inference}),
    the threshold introduces no real arbitrariness, because the learned
    activations are strongly bimodal --- concentrated near $0$ and $1$, with
    little mass in between, as quantified in \cref{sec:exp:results}
    --- so the count is insensitive to the exact
    cut-off, and $\flatfrac{1}{2}$ was chosen as a sensible threshold for an
    activation probability.

    \paragraph{$\ell_{0}$ count} The $\ell_{0}$ pseudo-norm represents the
    amount of information the decoder has access to in order to reconstruct the
    input. This amount corresponds to the number of non-zero scalars it can
    reconstruct from. For a TopK \ac{SAE}, this number is its $k$ hyperparameter
    by construction;\footnote{See
    \cref{fn:exp:metrics:topk}.}
    for \iac{VAEE} it is the number of active concepts $\kappa$ multiplied by
    the embedding dimension of each concept $d$.

    For a fair comparison it is important to consider both metrics, since
    \iac{VAEE} that activates fewer \textit{concepts} than \iac{SAE} may still
    activate more \textit{neurons} per sample. $\kappa$ is therefore the fair
    unit for comparing the two models on interpretability, while $\ell_{0}$ is
    the unit of choice for comparing decoder capacity.
    \Cref{tab:exp:metrics}
    recaps the formulas used to compute $\kappa$ and $\ell_{0}$ for both models.

    \begin{table}[tb]
        \myfloatalign
        \begin{tabular}{lll}
            \toprule
            \tableheadline{Metric} & \tableheadline{SAE} & \tableheadline{VAEE} \\
            \midrule
            $\kappa$ (active concepts) & $\#\{k : z_{k}(\vb{x}) > 0\}$ & $\#\{k : \alpha_k(\vb{x}) \ge \flatfrac{1}{2}\}$ \\
            $\ell_{0}$ (active neurons) & $\kappa$ & $\kappa \cdot d$ \\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \caption[Sparsity metrics for \ac{SAE} and \ac{VAEE}]{

\section{Model Training} \label{sec:exp:training}

Unless stated otherwise, the \ac{VAEE} uses the shallow configuration of
\cref{sec:exp:results:ablation}: a single
linear-plus-\acs{GELU} layer for the encoder $f_\phi$ and a linear decoder
$g_\theta$, with cosine similarity for the gate
(\cref{eq:methods:proba:sim}). The embedding posterior
variance is fixed at $\sigma_0 = 0.1$ during training; at inference time the
sampling is dropped and the embedding $\vb{z}_{k}$ is set to its mean value
$\vb{\mu}_{k}$. The Gumbel-Sigmoid relaxation
(\cref{eq:methods:proba:gumbel}) uses temperature
$\tau_g = 0.5$ during training; at inference the relaxation is dropped and the
gate $c_{k}$ set to its posterior mean $\alpha_{k}$.

The \ac{VAEE} is trained to minimise the objective of
\cref{eq:methods:loss:objective} --- reconstruction error,
batch-wise sparsity \ac{KL}, entropy binarisation, and the conditional-embedding
\ac{KL}. The loss weights are set to $\beta = 4$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}} =
\gamma = 1$. The Bernoulli prior rate $\pi$ is the \ac{VAEE}'s sparsity dial,
swept to trace its frontier, but it controls sparsity only indirectly, and
$\tilde{\kappa} = \pi K$ is a target the optimisation is biased toward, not a
hard constraint. The final per-sample count $\kappa$ runs higher than the
nominal $\tilde{\kappa}$, since reconstruction drives $\kappa$ towards higher
values.

The TopK \ac{SAE} baseline is trained with its standard objective ---
reconstruction error under the top-$k$ activation plus auxiliary loss --- on the
same activations.

Both models were trained using the \texttt{Adam} optimiser
\cite{kingmaAdamMethodStochastic2017} with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ for
3\,000 epochs and an early stopping patience of 500 epochs.

\section{Results} \label{sec:exp:results}

\subsection{Quantitative results} \label{sec:exp:results:quant}

\Cref{fig:exp:kappa-frontier}
plots validation \ac{MSE} against the number of active concepts $\kappa$,
sweeping each model's sparsity dial. The models are matched on parameter count
(\cref{sec:exp:baselines}). Across the swept range
every \ac{VAEE} frontier lies below every \ac{SAE} frontier: at any given
concept count it reconstructs more faithfully, matching the best \ac{SAE}'s
reconstruction using roughly an order of magnitude fewer active concepts, and at
the dense end of its sweep it reaches a fidelity no \ac{SAE} attains, even at $k
= 128$. A few vector-valued concepts carry reconstruction further than many more
scalar atoms --- the relaxation of the sparsity--reconstruction trade-off the
architecture was designed to deliver.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \myfloatalign
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{gfx/kappa_vs_mse_frontier.png}
    \caption[VAEE vs SAE frontier: active concepts]{
        Sparsity--reconstruction frontier in active concepts $\kappa$, the unit
        relevant to interpretability; each model sweeps its sparsity dial at
        matched parameter count --- the \ac{SAE} over $k \in \{8, 32, 128\}$ and
        the \ac{VAEE} over $\tilde{\kappa} = \pi K \in \{4, 8, 32\}$. All
        \ac{VAEE} frontiers lie below every \ac{SAE} frontier --- with the
        \ac{VAEE} matching the best \ac{SAE}'s reconstruction at far fewer
        active concepts.
    }
    \label{fig:exp:kappa-frontier}
\end{figure}

This advantage is specific to the concept axis. \Cref{fig:exp:l0-frontier}
repeats the comparison in active \textit{neurons} $\ell_{0}$ --- the
decoder-capacity unit
(\cref{sec:exp:metrics}) --- where the two
families do not even occupy the same range. Because each active \ac{VAEE}
concept is a vector of width $d = 64$, every \ac{VAEE} operating point sits
above $\ell_{0} \approx 300$, while the \acp{SAE}, with scalar atoms, never
exceed $\ell_{0} = 128$; a head-to-head reading at matched $\ell_{0}$ is not
available from these runs. The trend is nonetheless clear: the \ac{SAE} reaches
a given reconstruction error with fewer active neurons, and the \ac{VAEE} buys
its low concept count by activating more neurons per sample. The trade-off is
not eliminated but relocated, from the number of concepts a reader inspects to
the number of scalars the decoder draws on. Which unit to privilege depends on
the purpose: interpretation proceeds concept by concept, never coordinate by
coordinate, so $\kappa$ is the axis on which the comparison bears --- and there
the \ac{VAEE} wins.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \myfloatalign
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{gfx/l0_vs_mse_frontier.png}
    \caption[VAEE vs SAE frontier: active neurons]{
        The sparsity--reconstruction frontier in active neurons $\ell_{0}$, the
        decoder-capacity unit, over the same sweeps as
        \cref{fig:exp:kappa-frontier}
        --- the \ac{SAE} over $k \in \{8, 32, 128\}$ and the \ac{VAEE} over
        $\tilde{\kappa} = \pi K \in \{4, 8, 32\}$. The families occupy disjoint
        ranges --- \acp{SAE} below $\ell_{0} = 128$, \acp{VAEE} above $\ell_{0}
        \approx 300$ --- so no matched-$\ell_{0}$ comparison is available; the
        \ac{SAE} reaches a given error with fewer active neurons.
    }
    \label{fig:exp:l0-frontier}
\end{figure}


Both metrics presuppose that the active-concept count is well defined: that
the continuous inference gate behaves, in practice, as a hard one.
\Cref{fig:exp:alpha-distribution}
bears this out: the validation-set distribution of the gate values $\alpha_{k}$
is sharply bimodal, almost all of its mass falling within $0.01$ of $0$ or $1$,
the extremes one to two orders of magnitude more frequent than any intermediate
value. The active-concept count is therefore insensitive to the exact cut-off,
and $\flatfrac{1}{2}$ is a natural threshold for an activation probability.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \myfloatalign
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{gfx/alpha_val_dist.png}
    \caption[Distribution of VAEE gate activations]{
        Validation-set distribution of the $128 \times 64$ \ac{VAEE} gate values
        $\alpha_{k}$. The mass is sharply bimodal, concentrated within $0.01$ of
        $0$ and $1$ (each bin spans $0.01$), the extreme bins one to two orders
        of magnitude more frequent than the interior --- so the gate is
        effectively hard at inference and the active-concept count is
        insensitive to the $\flatfrac{1}{2}$ threshold.
    }
    \label{fig:exp:alpha-distribution}
\end{figure}
